I was born on September 19, 1930, at St. John’s Hospital in Yonkers, New York. This was right around the time of the Great Depression, which began in 1929 and continued to affect the country deeply all the way through World War II. The United States entered the war in 1941, after the attack on Pearl Harbor. I don’t remember the specific historical events of that time—as I was only 13 years old—but the war certainly had a big impact on my life. These memories, after all, are from the perspective of a child.

My mother had a history of physical problems. When she was about 16, she contracted rheumatic fever. At that time, there were no antibiotics—those didn’t come into use until around World War II. Because of that, when her body tried to fight off the high fever, her heart swelled from the strain. Although she survived—unlike many others with the illness—her heart was left with scar tissue. That damage caused circulatory issues that affected her for the rest of her life.

This brings me to an important chapter in my childhood. At one point during the war, my mother became very ill and needed special care. While she was being treated, other family members stepped in to take care of me—and that’s where my Cuban story begins.

(1944) I was around 13 years old at the time. My aunt Edna and uncle Roy were living in Cuba, where he was stationed as the military attaché for the Air Force at the American Embassy. They were also my godparents, so I went to stay with them for a few months. My aunt and I first took a train down to Miami, Florida, and from there we flew in a small plane to Havana, Cuba.

Edna and Roy had three children—my cousins Charlie, Joan, and Janine. I remember having a wonderful time living with them. This was during the rule of Fulgencio Batista, who was a dictator, not a democratically elected leader. Cuban society at the time was extremely divided—some were incredibly wealthy, and many were desperately poor.

I still remember the stark contrast. Driving down the main streets, you’d see grand pavilions and beautiful architecture. But just turn down a side street, and you'd witness heartbreaking scenes: children begging, some missing fingers or toes, crying for food or help. If you parked your car somewhere, you'd often have to pay a group of children to watch it—five or six of them sometimes—or risk coming back to find it damaged. That was the kind of life many people there faced.

Despite those harsh realities, most of my days in Cuba were full of adventure and joy. We spent time at country clubs along the seashore, swimming and diving, collecting gems from the ocean floor, and inventing games to play. I also had to attend school—but everything was in Spanish, which I didn’t speak. Still, I did well in English class and felt proud of that.

Since my uncle was part of the embassy, they socialized with the upper class of Cuban society. One visit I especially remember was to a wealthy family’s sugar plantation. It was so large it had its own railway system! Janine and I would ride horses around the grounds, spending the day exploring with a packed lunch and drinks. I don’t remember if anyone supervised us, but I do remember the glorious sense of freedom we felt. This usually happened when we were headed inland, toward Guantanamo, where the U.S. military base was. We’d go to the commissary there to shop for food and supplies—though what I remember most was the food.

Eventually, it was time to return home to the United States. When I traveled to Cuba, I was with my aunt. But going home, I traveled alone. I flew back to Miami and was supposed to take a train back to New York City—a trip that would take at least two days, with an overnight stay. My aunt suggested that during the layover in Miami, I take a taxi to a movie theater to pass the time. But I was nervous I might miss my train, so instead, I went straight to the train station.

No one was supervising me. I was just 13 years old, alone in a city far from home. I remember standing outside the station watching a Pepsi or Coca-Cola bottling demonstration through a window. I spent quite a while there, though I don’t know exactly how long. Eventually, I got on the correct train heading north toward New York.

During the ride, I made friends with a man I thought of as elderly—though looking back, he was probably just over thirty. He was a soldier, wearing a uniform, and he was kind and friendly. I remember that he told me, "If your father isn’t there to meet you when you arrive in New York, find a policeman."

I still have the train ticket from that trip. When I look back on it now, I’m amazed that a 13-year-old managed such a journey—flying from Cuba, navigating taxis, finding the train, and riding it all the way up the East Coast to New York City. But things were different back then.

I remember my time in Cuba so vividly. Oddly, I don’t recall worrying about my mother. I don’t think I was told how sick she was. I had thought I’d be gone for just a week or two, not months. It was the only time in my childhood I was separated from her for so long.

Looking back, my journey to Cuba as a young girl during World War II remains one of the most defining experiences of my childhood. What began as a necessary separation from my ailing mother turned into an eye-opening adventure that shaped my understanding of the world. I witnessed both privilege and poverty, navigated unfamiliar places, and learned to rely on my own judgment at a young age. Though I didn’t fully grasp the seriousness of my mother’s illness at the time, the experience gave me a deeper resilience and a lasting appreciation for the family who cared for me—and for the broader world I got to see, if only briefly, through a child’s eyes.
